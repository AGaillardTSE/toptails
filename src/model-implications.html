Implications for heterogeneous-agent models

In the data, the upper tails rank from thinnest to thickest as consumption, labor income, wealth, and capital income (see United States – Tails by quantile and Across Countries – Tails by quantile). In Gaillard et al. (2026), we show that the canonical heterogeneous agents (HA) model with homothetic, wealth-independent preferences and scale-independent returns predicts equally thick tails for consumption, wealth, and capital income. Intuitively, under the permanent income hypothesis, households smooth consumption according to their lifetime resources. At very high wealth, assets dominate those resources, so consumption becomes proportional to wealth. With scale-independent returns, capital income also scales in proportion to wealth. Homothetic heterogeneous-agent models, including models with entrepreneurs, can use differences in returns across households to address the wealth concentration puzzle by matching observed top wealth shares or the wealth Gini. The same mechanism can make consumption too concentrated at the top, even more so than labor income, giving rise to the consumption concentration puzzle.

We show that non-homothetic, wealth-dependent preferences and scale-dependent returns can reproduce both the ranking and the magnitudes of the observed Pareto tail coefficients.

Identification from Pareto coefficients

We show that, in a model with strongly wealth-dependent preferences, two ratios of asymptotic Pareto coefficients identify the relative curvature of preferences and the scale dependence of returns. A thicker capital-income tail than wealth tail implies a positive η in the model. At a given return shock, a positive η means that the rate of return rises with invested wealth.

Under the conditions of Corollary 1, the upper-tail elasticity of consumption with respect to wealth at a given age is ν/γ. A larger γ/ν means consumption rises more slowly as wealth increases (the consumption function is more concave in wealth).

Example. A consumption-to-wealth tail ratio of 2.0 and a capital-income-to-wealth ratio of 0.85 imply η ≈ 0.18 and γ/ν ≈ 2.35 in the asymptotic model.

Quantitative results

In the standard Aiyagari calibration, the top 1% hold 3% of wealth, compared with 35% in the data. We apply the same tail estimation procedure to the data and to simulated samples comparable to the PSID. The full model closely matches the ordering and levels of all four tail coefficients.

With γ fixed at 2, the full model estimates ν = 0.844 and η = 0.0815. The consumption ratio strongly disciplines preference curvature. At finite wealth levels, persistent differences in returns across investors can also thicken the capital-income tail, so the quantitative fit only weakly separates return type from return scale.

In the full model, the marginal propensity to consume out of wealth falls from about 7% between the 70th and 80th wealth percentiles to 2% in the top 1%. The model also needs less return heterogeneity to match wealth concentration. Entrepreneurs in the top 1% earn about 22%, compared with 34% in the homothetic specification. Sections 4.3–4.4
