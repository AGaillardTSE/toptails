Implications for heterogeneous-agent models

In the data, the upper tails rank from thinnest to thickest as consumption, labor income, wealth, and capital income (see United States – Tails by quantile and Across Countries – Tails by quantile). In Gaillard et al. (2026), we show that the canonical heterogeneous agents (HA) model with homothetic, wealth-independent preferences and scale-independent returns predicts equally thick tails for consumption, wealth, and capital income. Intuitively, under the permanent income hypothesis, households smooth consumption according to their lifetime resources. At very high wealth, assets dominate those resources, so consumption becomes proportional to wealth. With scale-independent returns, capital income also scales in proportion to wealth. The standard Aiyagari model struggles to match observed top wealth shares or the wealth Gini, a difficulty known as the wealth concentration puzzle. Homothetic heterogeneous-agent models, including models with entrepreneurs, can address this puzzle by allowing returns to differ across households. The same mechanism can make consumption too concentrated at the top, even more concentrated than labor income, giving rise to a mirror-image consumption concentration puzzle.

We show that non-homothetic, wealth-dependent preferences and scale-dependent returns can reproduce both the ranking and the magnitudes of the observed Pareto tail coefficients.

Main identification result from Pareto coefficients

We show that, in a model with strongly wealth-dependent preferences, two ratios of asymptotic Pareto coefficients identify the degree of non-homotheticity in preferences and the scale dependence of returns. A thicker capital-income tail than wealth tail implies a positive η in the model. At a given return shock, a positive η means that the rate of return rises with invested wealth.

ζcζa =γν(1+η) ⇒ γν=ζcζra

Under the conditions of Corollary 1, the upper-tail elasticity of consumption with respect to wealth at a given age is ν/γ. A larger γ/ν means consumption rises more slowly as wealth increases (the consumption function is more concave in wealth).

Example. A consumption-to-wealth tail ratio of 2.0 and a capital-income-to-wealth ratio of 0.85 imply η ≈ 0.18 and γ/ν ≈ 2.35 in the asymptotic model.

Quantitative results

In the standard Aiyagari calibration, the top 1% hold 3% of wealth, compared with 35% in the data. This gap illustrates the wealth concentration puzzle and motivates extensions that can generate greater wealth inequality. Within the same life-cycle economy, we compare a canonical homothetic benchmark with our full model. We apply the same Pareto estimation procedure to the adjusted PSID and to simulated samples designed to mirror the survey sample.

Homothetic benchmark. Households derive utility from consumption alone, and labor earnings have a Pareto upper tail. Entrepreneurs earn higher returns than workers. Within each group, the return rate is independent of wealth.

Full model. Wealth enters utility directly, and its marginal utility declines more slowly than that of consumption. This strengthens saving incentives at the top. The full model retains differences in returns across households and lets the return rate rise with invested wealth.

Reading the consumption concentration puzzle. The highlighted KS ratios show that the homothetic model makes consumption more concentrated than labor income (1.33 versus 1.46), since a lower coefficient means a thicker tail. The data put consumption below labor income in concentration (2.08 versus 1.43). The full model reproduces that ordering and closely matches the observed ratios.
