Implications for heterogeneous-agent models

In the data, the upper tails rank from thinnest to thickest as consumption, labor income, wealth, and capital income. The canonical model with homothetic, wealth-independent preferences and scale-independent returns predicts equally thick tails for consumption, wealth, and capital income.

Identification from Pareto coefficients

In the paper’s asymptotic model with strongly wealth-dependent preferences, two tail ratios identify the relative curvature of preferences and the scale dependence of returns. Here ζc is the coefficient for consumption, ζa for invested wealth before terminal bequests, and ζra for capital income. Section 3.3, Corollary 1

Capital income and wealth identify return scale dependence

ζraζa =11+η ⇒ η=ζaζra−1

Holding the return shock fixed, the rate of return scales with invested wealth as aη. A thicker capital-income tail than wealth tail implies a positive η in the model.

The two ratios identify relative preference curvature

ζcζa =γν(1+η) ⇒ γν=ζcζra

γ and ν govern how quickly marginal utility falls with consumption and wealth, respectively. Once return scale dependence is accounted for, a larger γ/ν means consumption rises more slowly with wealth at a given age in the upper tail.

Example. A consumption-to-wealth tail ratio of 2.0 and a capital-income-to-wealth ratio of 0.85 imply η ≈ 0.18 and γ/ν ≈ 2.35 in the asymptotic model.

Quantitative results

In the standard Aiyagari calibration, the top 1% hold 3% of wealth, compared with 35% in the data. Adding entrepreneurs with heterogeneous returns brings the wealth tail close to the data while leaving consumption too concentrated. A direct motive to hold wealth allows the full model to match all four tails in simulated samples comparable to the PSID.
