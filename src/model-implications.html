Implications for heterogeneous-agent models

In the data, the upper tails rank from thinnest to thickest as consumption, labor income, wealth, and capital income (see United States – Tails by quantile and Across Countries – Tails by quantile). In Gaillard et al. (2026), we show that the canonical heterogeneous agents (HA) model with homothetic, wealth-independent preferences and heterogeneous scale-independent returns predicts equally thick tails for consumption, wealth, and capital income. Intuitively, under the permanent income hypothesis, households smooth consumption according to their lifetime resources. At very high wealth, assets dominate those resources, so consumption becomes proportional to wealth. With scale-independent returns, capital income also scales in proportion to wealth.

As a consequence, when homothetic heterogeneous-agent models, including models with entrepreneurs, successfully addressed the wealth concentration puzzle (the inability of the standard Aiyagari model with heterogeneous labor income to reproduce the observed concentration of wealth), they also made consumption too concentrated at the top, giving rise to a mirror consumption concentration puzzle.

We show that non-homothetic, wealth-dependent preferences and scale-dependent returns can reproduce both the ranking and the magnitudes of the observed Pareto tail coefficients.

Main identification result from Pareto coefficients

A thicker capital-income tail than wealth tail implies a positive η in the model. At a given return shock, a positive η means that the rate of return rises with invested wealth.

Under the conditions of Corollary 1, the upper-tail elasticity of consumption with respect to wealth at a given age is ν/γ. A larger γ/ν means consumption rises more slowly as wealth increases (the consumption function is more concave in wealth).

Example. A consumption-to-wealth tail ratio of 2.0 and a capital-income-to-wealth ratio of 0.85 imply η ≈ 0.18 and γ/ν ≈ 2.35 in the asymptotic model.

Quantitative results

In the standard Aiyagari calibration, the top 1% hold 3% of wealth, compared with 35% in the data. This wealth concentration puzzle motivates extensions that can generate greater wealth inequality. Within the same life-cycle economy, we compare two models that solve this puzzle, (i) a canonical homothetic model and (ii) our full model with non-homothetic preferences and scale-dependent returns. We apply the same Pareto estimation procedure to the adjusted PSID and to simulated samples designed to mirror the survey sample.

Homothetic benchmark. Households derive utility from consumption alone (κ=0), and labor earnings have a Pareto upper tail. Entrepreneurs earn higher returns than workers (rH>rL). Within each group, the return rate is independent of wealth (η=0).

Full model. Wealth enters utility directly (κ>0), and its marginal utility declines more slowly than that of consumption (γ>ν). This strengthens saving incentives at the top. The full model retains differences in returns across types (rH>rL) and lets the return rate rise with invested wealth within each type (η>0).

Reading the consumption concentration puzzle. The homothetic model predicts that consumption is more concentrated at the top than labor income. At the very top, it predicts that consumption becomes as concentrated as wealth. In the data, consumption is less concentrated than both. The full model comes much closer to matching that pattern.
