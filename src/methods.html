Full algorithm: joint rescaling and reweighting

Let S denote the study survey (PSID or another dataset), and R the comparable reference survey (SCF for the US benchmark). Let x index matched income components, Ax their national-account totals, and w and v the study and reference weights. Amounts and targets use compatible definitions, years and price units.

  1. Initialize

    Set every rescaling factor to one and start from the original survey weights:

    λSx = λRx = 1 for every x;   wi = widesign,   vh = vhdesign.

    Keep the reference survey’s design weights fixed throughout.

  2. Rescaling: match national-account totals in both surveys

    Using the current study weights, calculate the factors that would match each component to its corresponding aggregate:

    λ̂Sx = ASx / Σi wi ySix

    λ̂Rx = ARx / Σh vh yRhx

    In the PSID baseline, move the study factors halfway toward these ratios: λSx ← 0.5λSx + 0.5λ̂Sx. Rescale SCF separately using its own ratios. Its factors can be computed once because its weights and aggregate targets remain fixed.

  3. Put both income distributions on a median-relative scale

    Construct the rescaled income index in each survey and divide it by that survey’s own current weighted median:

    zSi = Σx λSxySix,   uSi = zSi / medw(zS)

    zRh = Σx λRxyRhx,   uRh = zRh / medv(zR)

    Why normalize by the median?

    Rescaling anchors monetary levels to national accounts; reweighting matches the distribution of relative incomes. Without a common scale normalization, an income-level difference between surveys could be attributed either to rescaling amounts or to changing population weights.

    Dividing by each survey’s median removes a common positive monetary multiplier: (az) / med(az) = z / med(z). This fixes the scale for the distribution comparison. The median is also less sensitive to extreme incomes. Different component factors can still change relative incomes, which is why the two steps remain intertwined.

  4. Compare population shares in the same relative-income bins

    Use the reference survey to define bins Bj on the median-relative income scale. In each bin, compare the study share with the reference share:

    Mj = Σi wi 1{uSi ∈ Bj} / Σi wi

    πj = Σh vh 1{uRh ∈ Bj} / Σh vh

    The bins must have study observations wherever the reference has positive mass; supported coarsening and unavailable cases are documented in Data.

  5. Reweighting: update the study weights

    For a study household in bin Bj, the target update is:

    w̃i = wi × πj / Mj.

    Move partway toward this update for stability, and renormalize to preserve the original population total:

    winew = C [(1 − ρ)wi + ρw̃i].

    Here ρ is the damping rate. C is the common normalization multiplier that makes the new weights add up to the original design-weight total:

    C = Σi widesignΣi [(1 − ρ)wi + ρw̃i],   so   Σi winew = Σi widesign.

    The same C multiplies every household’s provisional weight, so it restores the total population weight without changing relative weights.

  6. Return to rescaling with the new weights

    Replace w by wnew and return to Step 2. Recalculate component totals, rescaling factors, weighted medians and income-bin shares. Continue until both the relative factor changes and relative bin-mass gaps are below the convergence tolerance (10−5 in the PSID baseline).

    At convergence, both sets of constraints hold together. Use the final weights to rescale the remaining income components, consumption and wealth. The PSID baseline’s iterative ranking index uses wages and business plus financial income; the other budget components are handled afterward.

When the benchmark is an external distribution such as WID rather than a comparable microdata survey, use its documented relative-income brackets and population shares in Step 4.

Income is normalized by its weighted median: ui = Σk λkyik / medω(Σk λkyik). Mj = Σi ωi1{ui ∈ Bj} / Σi ωi. The implementation damps the updates and preserves total population weight.

With the converged weights, PSID consumption categories match NIPA, household-funded health spending and Giving USA; its wealth components match the Financial Accounts. CEX follows the Meyer–Sullivan consumption construction, augmented with the PSID health, education, charitable-giving, income and wealth definitions. Its observed wealth components use controls with corresponding asset coverage on the final-interview wealth sample. SCF weights are not reweighted: its reporting-rescaled and fully adjusted versions coincide.
