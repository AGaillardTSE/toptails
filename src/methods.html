Rescaling and reweighting

This procedure follows Section 2.3 of the paper. The iterative algorithm is explained below.

Using the current observation weights ωi, determine a rescaling factor λk for each consumption, income or wealth category k. Multiply the reported amount yik for observation i by λk so that its weighted survey total matches the national-account target Yk. The rescaled incomes are then used for reweighting.

Formally, for category k and weights ω:

λk(ω)=Yk∑iωiyik

The survey’s population share in bin j is Mj(λ,ω), and the benchmark share in that same bin is πj. Update each observation’s weight from ωi to ωi′ using the benchmark share divided by the survey share.

Full algorithm: joint rescaling and reweighting

Let S denote the study survey and R the dataset providing the benchmark distribution. For observation i and category k, yik is a reported amount, Yk its national-account target, ωi its current weight and λk its rescaling factor. Superscripts S and R identify the datasets, and 0 denotes original design weights. When benchmark microdata are available, their observations are indexed by h. The formulas for R below apply to these microdata. When R provides published distributional data, such as WID or IRS income statistics, use its relative-income brackets and population shares πj directly in Step 4.

In the steps below, we repeatedly rescale the income components used to assign households to income bins, then update their weights to match the benchmark distribution. Once the weights and rescaling factors stabilize, we use the final weights to rescale the remaining consumption, income and wealth categories to their national-account totals.

  1. Initialize weights and factors

    Start from the original study-survey weights and set every rescaling factor to one. If the benchmark is constructed from microdata, keep its original observation weights fixed throughout.

    ωiS=ωiS,0,λkS=λkR=1

  2. Rescale amounts to national accounts

    Using the current weights, calculate the factor that matches each ranking-income category to its account target. Do this separately for the survey data and, where applicable, the benchmark microdata:

    λkS^=YkS∑iωiSyikS
    λkR^=YkR∑hωhRyhkR
    Updates are damped for numerical stability:λkS←0.5λkS+0.5λkS^

  3. Express income relative to its weighted median

    Add the rescaled income components to obtain income z, then divide by the survey’s own weighted median to obtain relative income u.

    ziS=∑kλkSyikS,uiS=ziSmedωS(zS)
    zhR=∑kλkRyhkR,uhR=zhRmedωR(zR)

    Why divide by the median? If all incomes double, the median doubles too, leaving relative incomes unchanged. Reweighting therefore corrects population shares across relative-income bins, rather than a common gap in reported income levels.

  4. Compare population shares in the same income bins

    Use the benchmark’s relative-income brackets to define common bins indexed by j. Let IijS equal one when study observation i is in bin j, and zero otherwise; IhjR is the corresponding benchmark-data indicator.

    PSID example. The benchmark bins are P0–P50, P50–P70, P70–P80, P80–P85, P85–P90, P90–P95, P95–P97.5, P97.5–P99, P99–P99.5 and P99.5–P100. These are benchmark percentiles. Their income cutoffs, divided by the benchmark median, define the same relative-income bins in PSID.

    Mj(λ,ω)=∑iωiSIijS∑iωiS
    πj=∑hωhRIhjR∑hωhR

    Mj(λ,ω) depends on the current rescaling factors and weights. πj is the benchmark share. Each bin with a positive benchmark share must contain study observations.

  5. Reweight observations using the bin-share ratio

    For each study observation i in bin j, multiply its current weight by the benchmark share divided by the survey share, so weights increase in under-represented bins and decrease in over-represented bins:

    ωiS~=ωiSπjMj(λ,ω)

    For stability, move a fraction ρ toward this target, then normalize the new weights to preserve the total population:

    ωiS,new=∑iωiS,0∑i[(1−ρ)ωiS+ρωiS~]⏟Preserves the total population×[(1−ρ)ωiS+ρωiS~]⏟Damped weight update

  6. Return to rescaling and check convergence

    Use the new study weights and return to Step 2. Recalculate the rescaling factors, weighted median and bin shares. Continue until both the relative factor changes and the gaps between survey and benchmark bin shares meet the convergence tolerance (10−5 in the PSID baseline).

    With the converged weights, rescale the remaining consumption, income and wealth categories.

At convergence, the final weights ωi* and rescaling factors λk* make PSID match the NIPA aggregates and the benchmark population shares across the common bins.
